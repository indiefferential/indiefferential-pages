Twenty-Five Years of Bedroom Noise, Finally Turned Up

Every rock dream starts somewhere small. For Into Twelve, it started in unfledged student bands in the 1990s, and it was kept alive through twenty-five years of music reveries, bedroom noodling and passionate songwriting. Home studio technology eventually made the dream possible, and the result is a DIY indie rock project with a wide and well-chosen map of influences. Its latest single, “Summer,” opens a new and more intimate chapter. The song was produced by Andy Savours, a mix engineer for My Bloody Valentine, and it carries his signature blend of noise and clarity while keeping the radical rock essence that defines the project. That balance is a delicate one. Noise without clarity turns to mud, and clarity without noise loses its edge, so a producer who can hold both is a valuable partner for a band aiming at emotional intensity. The influences read like a love letter to indie rock history. Into Twelve aims to sit somewhere between the straightforwardness of the first Pixies records, the lo-fi tones of Guided by Voices, the lyricism of Arcade Fire, the anger of Fugazi and the loose mood of Pavement, with the sonic realms of Yo La Tengo and Fontaines DC in the background. Together those names describe a project that values directness, texture and feeling over polish. What stands out is the sincerity beneath the references. Plenty of acts borrow from the past, but this one frames its sound as the realization of a long-held dream, built by someone who has been writing and recording for decades. That patience gives the music an unforced character. It does not sound like a trend. It sounds like the product of real devotion, refined over many years of private work. The word intimate is important here. The single marks a quieter, more personal turn, yet it keeps the rock edge intact, suggesting a band that understands intensity can come from closeness as well as volume. The campaign around the song aims to bring wider exposure through music media outlets and specialized radio, giving it a chance to reach the listeners who most appreciate this heritage. There is generosity in that approach. The project does not hide its heroes, and it does not pretend to have arrived from nowhere. Instead it honors the sounds that shaped it while using home studio tools to build something personal, a mix of affection and independence that gives the record its character. For anyone who loves guitar music with history in its bones and noise in its heart, “Summer” is a warm and welcome find.
