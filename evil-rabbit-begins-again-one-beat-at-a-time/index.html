Evil Rabbit Begins Again, One Beat at a Time

By Mark Hale
August 13, 2026

The first step into a new creative role can reveal as much about an artist as years of finished work. For Evil Rabbit, that step arrives with "Tender," his first official release as a music producer and the opening single from an upcoming debut mixtape. Having only begun producing less than a year ago, he approaches the track not as a declaration that he has arrived, but as evidence of a process already underway.

That distinction matters. "Tender" comes from an artist still learning the language of production, experimenting with ideas, and discovering how the instincts he developed as a touring drummer can translate into a different kind of musical architecture. Rather than presenting the single as a polished endpoint, Evil Rabbit allows it to represent something more interesting, the beginning of a new chapter built through curiosity, collaboration, and repetition.

His background behind the drum kit provides an important foundation. As a touring drummer, Evil Rabbit has spent years understanding rhythm from the inside, learning how timing, dynamics, space, and physical movement can shape a performance. Production asks for a different kind of listening, but the instincts are connected. Where a drummer once responds to the movement of a band in real time, a producer has the opportunity to construct that movement piece by piece, deciding how every sound contributes to the final atmosphere.

That transition has happened remarkably quickly. Less than a year after stepping into production, Evil Rabbit is already introducing his first official material while building toward a larger body of work. Yet the speed of the progression does not appear to have replaced patience. If anything, "Tender" represents an artist who understands that learning the craft is part of the music itself.

The title carries an intriguing sense of vulnerability for a debut production. "Tender" suggests sensitivity, attention, and a willingness to handle something carefully. In the context of Evil Rabbit's current journey, it feels appropriate. He is entering unfamiliar territory while bringing years of musical experience with him, testing what happens when the drummer's instinct for rhythm becomes part of a broader production process.

Much of the character surrounding the release comes from the creative community he has developed alongside in Tucson. Working with a growing network of artists and creatives has allowed Evil Rabbit to learn through collaboration rather than isolation. That environment becomes particularly important for an emerging producer, where every session can introduce a new approach to arrangement, sound selection, recording, or songwriting.

There is an appealing sense of collective discovery to that process. Evil Rabbit is not presenting himself as an artist operating from a finished blueprint. He is building that blueprint as he goes, learning from the people around him and carrying each experience into the next record. The result is a creative trajectory that feels open ended, with "Tender" functioning as the first visible marker of a much longer road.

His decision to move from performance into production also reflects a natural expansion of musical curiosity. Drumming teaches an artist to understand the importance of groove, but producing requires attention to every layer surrounding it. Texture, atmosphere, arrangement, transitions, and sonic detail all become part of the composition. The challenge is not simply to make a beat work, but to understand why a particular sound belongs there and what happens when it is removed.

For someone with a background in touring, that shift can be especially revealing. Live performance is immediate and physical. Production is more solitary and microscopic, often demanding repeated listening and tiny adjustments that may be invisible to anyone outside the studio. Evil Rabbit's first year in the role has therefore been a period of learning how to move between those two worlds.

"Tender" represents that learning process in real time. It is the first step toward establishing an identity beyond the role of drummer, while still carrying the rhythmic instincts that brought him there in the first place. That connection gives the release a sense of continuity. He is not abandoning one musical identity for another. He is expanding the possibilities of what that identity can become.

The upcoming mixtape promises to make that development easier to trace. Rather than treating "Tender" as a standalone experiment, Evil Rabbit is using it as the beginning of a larger body of work through which he can test ideas, work with other artists, and refine his production instincts. Each release becomes another opportunity to learn, with the mixtape serving as a record of that evolution.

There is something refreshing about an artist being willing to occupy that space. Music culture often places enormous pressure on new artists to arrive fully formed, as though experimentation should happen privately and only the finished product should be presented to an audience. Evil Rabbit takes a different approach. The appeal of his current chapter lies partly in watching the craft develop.

That does not mean ambition is absent. Quite the opposite. The desire to collaborate with more artists and continue improving points toward a long term creative vision. The difference is that Evil Rabbit seems more interested in building something durable than rushing toward a particular destination.

Tucson provides the backdrop for that development, giving him access to a community of musicians and creatives whose influence can become part of the process. Local scenes have always played an important role in shaping distinctive artists, particularly when collaboration happens organically and people are willing to experiment outside established formulas. Evil Rabbit's current work appears to be emerging from exactly that kind of environment.

There is also a certain confidence in calling the first release "Tender." It is not a title that suggests aggression or self importance. Instead, it points toward emotional openness, a quality that can be surprisingly powerful in music made by someone entering a new phase. Production is often associated with control, but "Tender" hints at another side of the discipline, the ability to listen closely enough to know when an idea needs space.

That may ultimately become one of the defining qualities of Evil Rabbit's development. His experience as a drummer has already taught him that rhythm is not simply about playing more. It is about knowing when to strike, when to hold back, and how silence can shape momentum. Those instincts can become equally valuable in production, where restraint and timing often determine whether a track feels alive.

As a first official release, "Tender" is therefore best understood as a beginning rather than a conclusion. It introduces a producer who is still discovering the full extent of his capabilities, but who already has a substantial musical foundation beneath the experimentation. The upcoming mixtape will provide a broader canvas, allowing him to explore what happens when those instincts are pushed further.

For now, the most compelling part of Evil Rabbit's story is its openness. He is learning, collaborating, experimenting, and allowing each record to teach him something that the next one can build upon. "Tender" captures that spirit at an early but significant moment, when the possibilities still seem wide open and every new production choice carries the excitement of discovery.

The track may be the first official marker of Evil Rabbit's life as a producer, but it does not feel like a line drawn under his past. It feels like another door opening. Behind it is a drummer's understanding of rhythm, a new producer's curiosity about sound, and a growing Tucson creative community ready to contribute to the next chapter.

"Tender" is where that journey becomes audible. What comes next will show just how far Evil Rabbit can take it.

Mark Hale

Mark Hale is a writer, editor, and long-time cultural commentator specialising in guitars, music technology, and the quietly absurd world that surrounds them. He writes about instruments the way other people write about cars: with affection, irritation, and an unwavering belief that function matters more than mythology. Raised in a household where music was not a hobby but a constant presence, Hale grew up surrounded by vinyl records, half-functional amplifiers, and instruments that were always slightly out of tune. From an early age, he learned that guitars had personalities. Some were dependable. Some were brilliant but infuriating. Some looked magnificent and behaved like absolute idiots. This belief—that tools reveal character—has stayed with him and underpins his entire approach to writing. Before becoming a full-time writer, Hale spent years embedded in the unglamorous machinery of the music world. He worked in guitar shops where marketing promises collided daily with reality, learning very quickly which instruments survived real players and which existed purely for catalog photography. He loaded vans, wired pedalboards minutes before soundcheck, repaired cables with borrowed soldering irons, and watched outstanding musicians coax magic from battered gear while others struggled heroically with instruments that cost more than a small car.
